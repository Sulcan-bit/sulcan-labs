Crude oil pricing and calendar basis
Canadian pricing cadence:  
Canadian crude prices are quoted daily, but commercial deals are typically structured on a calendar‑month basis. Production volumes are also contracted month‑to‑month.

Units and currencies:  
Pricing moves fluidly between CAD and USD (usually using Bank of Canada FX rates), and between barrels and cubic meters.

Transaction points in the value chain
Crude can be bought and sold at three main locations:

Wellhead:  
At the battery–feeder pipeline junction.

Feeder pipeline:  
At the point where a feeder line connects into a trunk line.

Trunk pipeline:  
At the start or end of a major pipeline system.

Each point implies different quality and location adjustments.

Components of a crude price
A crude transaction price is typically built from:

Starting price:  
A reference price—either a company posting or a publicly quoted market price.

Quality adjustment:  
Adjusts for differences between the crude being traded and the reference crude (density, sulphur, etc.).

Location adjustment:  
Translates the starting price to the actual transaction location (e.g., Edmonton vs Hardisty vs Cromer).

Market adjustment:  
Reflects the negotiated premium or discount versus the “starting price,” based on market conditions.

Equalization (quality valuation system)
Equalization:  
A standardized framework used to value quality differences between crudes—primarily density and sulphur—so that trades can be normalized to a common reference.

Postings and reference vs stream prices
Posting:  
A publicly or privately stated price at which a company is willing to buy (or occasionally sell) a specific crude, at a defined location, with specified quality, over a given time period.

Reference prices:  
Prices for crudes with defined sulphur and density specs.

Stream prices:  
Prices for “as‑is” crudes—actual blended streams in the pipeline system.

Canadian posters:  
Not all posters quote the same crude type, quality, or location. Many are accessible via phone hotlines or company websites. Key Canadian posters include Flint Hills, Imperial Oil, and Suncor.

Edmonton Par and MSW
Edmonton Par:  
A Canadian light sweet reference crude, defined at 825 kg/m³ density and 0.3% sulphur. It is a key marker for Canadian light sweet pricing.

MSW (Mixed Sweet Blend):  
The physical stream corresponding to Edmonton Par.

“Mixed Sweet Blend (MSW) is the benchmark conventionally produced light sweet crude for western Canada. It is often referenced on crude price reports as Edmonton Par Crude. It is a fully blended aggregate of many feeder streams and only begins its existence in blend tanks in Edmonton, Alberta.”

NYMEX WTI and CMA pricing
NYMEX WTI futures:  
Light sweet crude oil futures traded on NYMEX; each contract is 1,000 bbl, with delivery at Cushing, Oklahoma. WTI (~825 kg/m³, 0.3% S) is a key U.S. light sweet marker and increasingly used in contract pricing.

Linkage to Canadian prices:  
Canadian crude prices are often tied to the settlement price of the nearby WTI futures contract (the “prompt” month).

Nearby month mechanics:  
The prompt month futures contract expires before the end of the calendar month; the next month then becomes the nearby contract.

Calendar Month Average (CMA):  
The arithmetic average of the nearby futures settlement prices for each business day in the calendar month.

WTI‑based pricing and differentials
Differential pricing:  
Canadian crudes trade at a premium or discount to WTI CMA. The differential reflects:

Transportation differences

Quality differences

Supply/demand dynamics

Example (Feb 2014):

MSW (light sweet at Edmonton): WTI – $5.07

LSB (light sour at Cromer, MB): WTI – $5.90

WCS (heavy blend at Hardisty): WTI – $19.14

Crude oil value drivers
Product slate:  
Crude value depends on the products that can be produced and the cost to produce them.

Primary valuation metrics (Canada):  
The Canadian industry largely uses density and sulphur as the primary measures of crude value.

Density:

Heavier crudes yield more low‑value products (asphalt, heavy fuel oil).

Lighter crudes yield more high‑value products (gasoline, diesel).

Sulphur:

Higher sulphur increases processing cost (desulphurization units, emissions controls).

Value reflects the supply/demand balance for those processing facilities.

Streams and WADF
Stream:  
The combined output of all volumes injected into a pipeline system.

The volume‑weighted average differential of all inputs to a stream. Used to translate between stream postings and reference postings.

Key relationships:

Stream Price + WADF = 40° Price

Reference Price + Quality Adj. to 825 kg/m³ / 0.5% S = 40° Price

40° Price:  
A widely used Canadian pricing concept that simplifies calculations by normalizing to a standard quality.

Cochin Condensate — Stream Overview
Cochin condensate is a pentanes‑plus (C5+) stream originating in the U.S. Midwest and transported into the Fort Saskatchewan, Alberta region via the Pembina Cochin pipeline system.
It is delivered on a continuous, rateable basis to condensate handling facilities in the Fort Sask area.

This stream is widely used as a diluent for heavy crude blending due to its light density, high pentane content, and stable supply profile.

1. Physical Properties (Most Recent & Historical Averages)
Density & Gravity
Density: ~659–658 kg/m³ (very light condensate)

API Gravity: ~83° API
Indicates a highly volatile, light hydrocarbon mixture suitable for diluent use.

Sulphur & MCR
Sulphur: ND (non‑detectable)

Micro Carbon Residue: ND
Cochin is effectively sulphur‑free and contains negligible residue, making it attractive for blending with heavy crude.

Viscosity
Viscosity @ 7.5°C: ~0.5–0.6 cSt
Extremely low viscosity, contributing to strong diluent performance.

Sediment
Sediment: ~50–80 ppmw
Low sediment content, typical for high‑quality condensate streams.

6. Key Takeaways for Sulcan Labs Agent
Cochin is a premium-quality condensate with extremely light density (~660 kg/m³).

High C5+ content (~95%) makes it ideal for heavy oil diluent blending.
